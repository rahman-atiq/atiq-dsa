AVL Trees
The tree that refuses to slouch.

A binary search tree is only fast if it stays short. Left alone, it slumps into a linked list and quietly turns your O(log n) into O(n). AVL trees fix their own posture — after every insert and every delete — using exactly four moves.

Here is the gap in the BST definition. The rules constrain order — and say nothing at all about shape. Both trees above are perfectly legal BSTs holding identical keys; the insertion order alone decided which one you got. At a million sorted keys that costs you up to 1,000,000 comparisons instead of about 20. So we need one extra rule — one the tree can check on itself and repair without ever disturbing the ordering. Compare it to what you just wrote down.

Vocabulary

Balance factor: one number per node.

Every node carries its own BF
The little pill on each node is its balance factor.

The formula

BF(node) = height(node.left) − height(node.right) // height of an empty subtree = 0 // height of a single leaf = 1

The one rule

A tree is an AVL tree when every node has

−10+1 legal ±2 rotate!

Positive BF means left-heavy. Negative means right-heavy. A BF of ±2 is not a crime — it is a work order.

The one true shape

Three keys — 10, 20, 30 — can only form one balanced BST. So no matter which order your student inserted them in, or which of the four cases fired, the tree has to land here. If your answer to a drill isn't the balanced shape, you didn't pick the wrong rotation — you performed the right one incorrectly.

The proof, visually

Rotation reshuffles height, never order.

Before — LL at z
In-order walk: T1 x T2 y T3 z T4
↺
rotateRight(z)
After
In-order walk: T1 x T2 y T3 z T4

Identical. Character for character. A rotation only ever re-parents the single subtree caught in the middle (T3 here) and swaps a parent with its child — and both of those moves keep the same left-to-right sequence. Everything shifts vertically; nothing shifts horizontally. That's why you can rotate as violently as you like and searching still works.

Pin this to your forehead

The whole decision, in one table.

z is the node you rotate at — the lowest node whose BF hit ±2 as you climbed back toward the root. In every diagram in this deck it's the red one. Above z, nothing gets touched.

"the child" always means y — z's child on the heavy side, never the other one: y = z.left when BF(z) = +2, y = z.right when BF(z) = −2. It's the amber node, and it's the same y as in y = z.left in the rotation code.

BF of z BF of the heavy child y CaseWhat you do
+2 +1y = z.left, left-heavy LLrotateRight(z) — one move
+2 −1y = z.left, right-heavy LRrotateLeft(y), then rotateRight(z)
−2 −1y = z.right, right-heavy RRrotateLeft(z) — one move
−2 +1y = z.right, left-heavy RLrotateRight(y), then rotateLeft(z)

Signs match → single

If z and y lean the same way (+2 with +1, or −2 with −1), z–y is a straight line: one rotation, taken at z.

Signs clash → double

If they lean opposite ways, z–y is an elbow: two rotations — straighten it at y first, then rotate at z.

That's it. Find z, look at y, compare the two signs, pick a row. Everything else on the next twelve slides is just this table under pressure.

Your turn now

Five flashcards. Same routine every time.

1 · Find the culprit

Compute balance factors bottom-up. Stop at the first node that hits ±2. Deeper nodes first — always.

2 · Name the case

From that node, two steps down the heavy side. LL, RR, LR or RL. Say it out loud before you draw anything.

3 · Rotate

Redraw the whole tree. Do not do this in your head — that's how the orphan subtree gets lost.

4 · Verify

Recompute every BF. Then read the tree in-order and confirm it comes out sorted.

The lesson of drill 3: a rotation is a local repair. You rotate at the lowest unbalanced node, and the subtree you fix gets shorter — which usually satisfies every ancestor for free. Act two

Deletion uses the same four rotations — and behaves worse.

What stays identical

The four cases. The two primitives. The naming rule. The orphan hand-off. You do not learn a single new rotation. Everything you drilled in act one still applies, unchanged.

What gets nastier

1. Insert fires at most one rotation, ever. Delete can fire one at every level — up to O(log n) rotations from a single removal.

2. The child can have BF = 0, which is impossible during insertion. You need one extra row in your table.

Why the cascade happens. An insertion makes a subtree taller, and one rotation shrinks it right back to its original height — so ancestors never notice anything happened, and you can stop immediately. A deletion makes a subtree shorter, and the rotation that fixes it leaves it shorter still. That shortening propagates upward, so the grandparent can break next, and its parent after that. Moral: after a delete, you must keep checking all the way to the root. After an insert, you can stop at the first fix.

The extra row

One new case: when the child is perfectly balanced.

BF of z BF of the heavy child y CaseAction
+2 +1y = z.left, left-heavy LLrotateRight(z)
+2 0y = z.left, balanced ← new LLrotateRight(z) — treat 0 like +1
+2 −1y = z.left, right-heavy LRrotateLeft(y), then rotateRight(z)
−2 −1y = z.right, right-heavy RRrotateLeft(z)
−2 0y = z.right, balanced ← new RRrotateLeft(z) — treat 0 like −1
−2 +1y = z.right, left-heavy RLrotateRight(y), then rotateLeft(z)

The whole delete algorithm

// 1. ordinary BST delete // two children? swap with // in-order successor, then // delete that node instead // 2. walk back up to the root for z in path(removed → root): update height(z) if |BF(z)| > 1: z = rebalance(z) // keep going! do not stop

Same z (lowest node with |BF| = 2) and same y (z's child on the heavy side) as the cheat sheet. Only the zero rows are new. And even they don't ask you to learn a new move — they just tell you which way to break the tie. Ties go to the single rotation.

Note what a BF-0 child means: both of 10's subtrees were the same height, so promoting 10 leaves the repaired subtree at the same height it had before — height 3 in, height 3 out. That's precisely why nothing cascaded here and the root never even noticed. When the child's BF is 0, the rotation is free and you can stop. Any other value, and the subtree shrinks — which is when ancestors start falling over. Which brings us to the boss.

Take a photo of this one

Everything, on one slide.

The loop

1. Do the normal BST insert or delete.
2. Walk back up toward the root.
3. At each node: update height, check BF.
4. If |BF| > 1, rotate using the table.
5. Insert → you may stop after one fix.
    Delete → keep going to the root.

The two primitives

rotateLeft(z): y=z.right; z.right=y.left; y.left=z; return y rotateRight(z): y=z.left; z.left=y.right; y.right=z; return y // always: update height(z) BEFORE height(y)

The four cases

LL+2 / (+1 or 0)rotateRight(z)
LR+2 / −1rotateLeft(z.left) then rotateRight(z)
RR−2 / (−1 or 0)rotateLeft(z)
RL−2 / +1rotateRight(z.right) then rotateLeft(z)

Signs match → single rotation. Signs clash → double, child first. BF 0 only happens on delete; treat it as a match.

Costs

search O(log n) insert O(log n), ≤1 rotation delete O(log n), up to O(log n) rotations height ≤ 1.44 log₂n
Learn from other people's pain

The five mistakes, ranked by how often I see them.

1 · Rotating at the root by reflex

You must rotate at the lowest node with |BF| > 1. Drill 3 punishes this specifically. Compute bottom-up, not top-down.

2 · Losing the orphan subtree

Every rotation re-parents exactly one subtree — y.left or y.right. Drop it and you've deleted nodes you never meant to touch. If your node count changed, this is why.

3 · Stopping too early after a delete

Insert → one rotation and you're safe. Delete → climb all the way to the root. The boss fight exists entirely to burn this in.

4 · Doing a single rotation on an elbow

If z and its child lean opposite ways, one rotation just mirrors the problem. Check the two signs before you pick up the pen.

5 · Forgetting to update heights

In code, a rotation that doesn't refresh the heights of z first, then y leaves every balance factor above it lying to you. The tree looks fine and silently degrades.
